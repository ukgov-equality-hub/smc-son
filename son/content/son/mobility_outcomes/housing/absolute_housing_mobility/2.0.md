Data on absolute housing mobility – the strength in the link in owning or renting between people in the UK and their parents. 

[TOC]

## Summary main findings

### Longitudinal data (1971 to 2021)
According to the ONS Longitudinal Study (ONS-LS), rates of upward housing mobility were high in the censuses up to 2001. Nearly two-thirds of young people who lived in rented homes when they were growing up became homeowners themselves. Since then, these rates have dropped substantially, falling to around one-third by 2021.

Downward housing mobility stayed low until 2001, but went up in the years that followed.

Differences in housing mobility persisted between men and women. In every period, young women were less likely than men to experience upward mobility and more likely to experience downward mobility. Between the 1991 and 2021 censuses, the gap in upward mobility went up from 2.1 to 4.8 percentage points. The gap in downward mobility went up from 1.9 to 4.1 percentage points.

### Other data (2016 to 2020)
According to findings from the ONS Wealth and Assets Survey (WAS), in the period from 2016 to 2020 combined: 

* 71% of people whose parents were homeowners also owned their own home
* 46% of people whose parents were not homeowners owned their own home

Women were less likely than men to own their own homes. Among people whose parents were homeowners, 64% of women and 75% of men owned their own home. Among people whose parents were not homeowners, 35% of women and 55% of men owned their own homes.

Disabled people were less likely than non-disabled people to own their own homes. Among people whose parents were homeowners, 61% of disabled people and 74% of non-disabled people owned their own home. Among people whose parents were not homeowners, 34% of disabled people and 53% of non-disabled people owned their own home.

Home ownership was lowest in London out of all UK regions. 60% of people in London whose parents were homeowners owned their own home, compared with 35% of people whose parents were not homeowners.

Among people whose parents were not homeowners, home ownership was highest in the South West (where 54% of people owned their own homes) and Wales (52%).

## About the data

### Data source
* Office for National Statistics (ONS) Longitudinal Study
* ONS Wealth and Assets Survey (WAS)

### Time period
* Longitudinal study: 1971 to 2021
* WAS: 2016 to 2020 (combined)

### Geographic area
UK

### What the data measures
#### Longitudinal data (1971 to 2021)
Housing tenure is treated as a binary measure. People’s housing status is classed as either:

* owner – including outright owners and those with a mortgage
* renter – including private and social housing sectors 

The data does not include people living in communal housing when they were surveyed. See [section 3.4 of our technical annex] for more information.

Absolute upward housing mobility is defined as the conditional probability of an individual moving from rented accommodation when they were growing up to owner-occupation as adults. 

Absolute downward housing mobility is defined as the conditional probability of an individual moving from owner-occupied accommodation when they were growing up to renting as adults. 

#### Previous analysis (2016 to 2020)
The data shows the relationship in home ownership between people aged 25 to 64 years in the UK and their parents.

### Things you need to know
The error bars show 95% confidence intervals. Read more about
[confidence intervals](/state-of-the-nation/about-our-analysis#confidence-intervals).

#### Longitudinal data (1971 to 2021)
The census does not directly measure people’s social backgrounds. It links censuses to identify people’s families in an earlier census when they were growing up and still living at home. This is done for specific birth cohorts. For example, early-career adults were aged 28 to 37 at the time of the destination census and have been linked with data for their parents from the census 20 years earlier. Mid-career adults were aged 38 to 47 and have been linked with data for their parents from the census 30 years earlier. See the technical annex for further details.

While the ONS-LS achieved high levels of linkage across censuses, this method means that recent migrants who were not living in England or Wales in the relevant earlier census cannot be linked. Recent migrants from countries outside England and Wales are therefore excluded.

The questions asked in the census about housing tenure, and their coding, differ across the relevant censuses.

Future analysis for State of the Nation will use the ONS-LS to analyse results by ethnic group and disability status.

#### Previous analysis (2016 to 2020)
Data is weighted using the WAS individual weights.

Data analysed by area uses the area where respondents live now, not where they grew up.

### Type of data
* Longitudinal data: administrative data
* WAS data: survey data
