Data on relative occupational-wage mobility – the strength of the link between people’s occupational wages as adults and their parents’ occupational wages.

Data on relative income mobility – the link between parents’ and children’s income over time.

The longitudinal data gives us a stronger picture of the change over time. The other dataset provides rich breakdowns by protected characteristics.

[TOC]

## Summary main findings

### Longitudinal data (1971 to 2021)

According to the ONS Longitudinal Study (ONS-LS), relative wage mobility among early-career workers (aged 28 to 37 years) went down up to 2001 – it went up between 2011 and 2021. Relative mobility for young women remains significantly worse than in 1991. This means their parents' wages are now a  stronger predictor of their own earnings than they had been 30 years earlier.

Among mid-career workers (aged 40 to 46 years), intergenerational occupational-wage persistence went up across the 3 earliest cohorts. This means adult occupational wages became more closely linked to the occupation- wages of their parents over this period.

### Other data (1991 to 2020)

Findings from the UK Household Longitudinal Study (UKHLS) and the New Earnings Survey (NES) show the link between parents’ and children's incomes has gotten stronger between 1991 and 2004, and remained at a similar level since.

The link is lower when the respondents’ income is measured during their 20s. It goes up in their 30s and 40s, before going down again at age 50.

## About the data

### Data source(s)

* ONS Longitudinal Study (1971 to 2021)
* New Earnings Survey (NES, 1991 to 2020)
* UK Household Longitudinal Study (UKHLS, 2009 to 2020) 

Previous data: 

* Study period 1991 to 2004 – Blanden and Machin (2008), based on National Child Development Study and Birth Cohort Study 1970
* Study period 2009 to 2016 – Rohenkohl (2019), based on linked British Household Panel Study and UK Household Longitudinal Study (UKHLS) data
* Study period 2020 – calculations based on the UKHLS 2009 and 2020

### Time period

ONS Longitudinal Study: 1971 to 2021

Previous data: 1991 to 2020

### Geographic area

ONS Longitudinal Study: England and Wales

Previous data: UK

### What the data measures

#### Longitudinal Study 

The data shows the strength of the relationship between the parents’ estimated occupational wages and those of their adult children.

As the study does not collect direct income data, an imputed occupational-wage metric was created. Occupational profiles were matched with external historic earnings data from the New Earnings Survey and the Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings. From these occupational-wage scores, members of the study and their families of origin could be given scores identifying their percentile position within the overall distribution for the relevant wave. By estimating the strength of association between the members’ percentile positions and those of their parents, a measure of intergenerational relative occupational-wage mobility was obtained. 

See the technical annex for more details.

#### Previous analysis 

The data shows the link between parents’ and children’s incomes at different ages, over time.

### Things you need to know

The error bars show 95% confidence intervals. Read more about [confidence intervals](https://social-mobility.data.gov.uk/about-our-analysis#confidence-intervals).

#### Longitudinal Study 

The census does not directly measure people’s social backgrounds, but by linking censuses it is possible to identify people’s families in an earlier census when they were growing up and still living at home. This linkage is done for specific birth cohorts. For example, early-career adults were aged 28 to 37 at the time of the destination census and have been linked with data for their parents from the census 20 years earlier. Mid-career adults were aged 38 to 47 and have been linked with data for their parents from the census 30 years earlier. See the technical annex for further details.

While the ONS-LS achieved high levels of linkage across censuses, this method means that recent migrants who were not living in England or Wales in the relevant earlier census cannot be linked. Recent migrants from countries outside England and Wales are excluded.

Occupational-wage mobility, as measured here, is different to income mobility. This is because the measure of occupational wages is based on the average wages of people in different occupations, not on the wages of individual members of the LS. 

The occupational-wage mobility research was carried out by Emma Gorman, Franz Buscha and Min Zhang from the University of Westminster under ESRC grant ES/X012190/1. We are very grateful to them for making their results available to the Social Mobility Commission and for their helpful advice.

See section 3.4 of our technical annex.

#### Previous analysis 

The data shows the link between parents’ and children’s incomes at different ages, over time.

The 1991 study measured parents’ household income and their children’s earnings at 33 years old.

The 2004 study measured parents’ household income and their children’s earnings at 34 years old.

The 2009 to 2016 study measured parents’ gross household income and their children’s gross household income at 25 years of age or older.

The 2020 study measured parent’s gross household income and their children’s gross personal income at 25 years of age or older.

There are differences between the data and methodology of the different studies, which makes comparisons between them difficult. The 1991 and 2004 studies are closest in methodology and safest to compare. The estimates are likely to be underestimates of the likely strength of the link between parents’ and children’s income.
