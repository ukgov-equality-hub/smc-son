## Summary main findings

### Longitudinal data (1971 to 2021)
According to the ONS Longitudinal Study (ONS-LS), upward educational mobility among people aged 28 to 37 from non-graduate families went up significantly from 1991 to 2011. This means that more young people were becoming the first generation in their families to earn a degree. The rate of growth has levelled off between 2011 and 2021.

In 1991, 11% of young men from non-graduate families obtained a degree compared to just 7% of young women. By 2001, upward mobility reached nearly 20% for both groups as women caught up with men. Women then moved ahead by 2011, with 37% gaining a degree compared to 32% of men. By 2021, women widened this lead even further.

Downward educational mobility among young people from graduate families fell continuously up to 2011, before rising again in 2021. This means fewer children of graduates were ending up without a degree until 2011, after which the trend reversed.

### Other data (1991 to 2020)
According to findings from the UK Household Longitudinal Survey (UKHLS), for people whose parents did not have a university degree:

* those from Chinese (64%), White Other (44%), Indian (40%) and Pakistani (37%) ethnic backgrounds were the most likely out of all ethnic groups to have a degree themselves
* those living in London (39%) and the South East (32%) were the most likely out of all regions to have a degree themselves – those living in the East Midlands (22%), Wales (23%), Yorkshire and the Humber (24%), and the West Midlands (26%) were least likely
* those with a long-term illness or disability were less likely to have a university degree than people without a disability from the same education background – they were also more likely to have no qualifications

## About the data

### Data source
* ONS Longitudinal Study (1971 to 2021)
* UK Household Longitudinal Survey (UKHLS) (1991 to 2020)

### Time period
* Longitudinal Study: 1971 to 2021
* UKHLS: 1991 to 2020

### Geographic area
UK

### What the data measures
#### Longitudinal data (1971 to 2021)
The data shows the relationship in education level between people and their parents.

#### UKHLS
The data measures absolute educational mobility among 25 to 64 year olds in the UK.

Upward absolute educational mobility is when someone has higher level qualifications than their parents. Downward mobility is when they have lower qualifications.

### Things you need to know
The error bars show 95% confidence intervals. Read more about
[confidence intervals](/state-of-the-nation/about-our-analysis#confidence-intervals).

#### Longitudinal data (1971 to 2021)
The census does not directly measure people’s social backgrounds. It links censuses to identify people’s families in an earlier census when they were growing up and still living at home. This is done for specific birth cohorts and a sample of birth dates. 

In the case of educational mobility we focus on young adults aged 28 to 37 at the time of the destination census and link their data with that for their parents from the census 20 years earlier. 

This method means that recent migrants who were not living in England or Wales in the relevant earlier census cannot be linked. Recent migrants from countries outside England and Wales are excluded.

The questions asked in the census about education, and their coding, differ across the relevant censuses but the census has consistently asked about degree-level qualifications. It is therefore possible to measure trends with respect to higher education consistently over time but not trends in school qualifications.

See section 3.4 of our technical annex for more information.

Future analysis for State of the Nation will use the ONS-LS to analyse results by ethnic group and disability status.

#### UKHLS
If respondents have more than one parent, the data measures the parent with the highest level of qualification.

Qualifications for respondents and their parents are divided into 4 categories:

* university degrees
* post-school qualifications
* school qualifications
* no qualifications

Data analysed by ethnicity and by area only includes respondents whose parents did not have a degree.

For data by disability status, respondents were asked if they had a physical or mental impairment, illness or disability for at least 12 months. This is different from Labour Force Survey data, which specifies that the condition should be both long-term and limiting. We do not know how long respondents had the illness or disability, and it is possible that they completed their education before the illness or disability occurred. Because of this, the data is likely to underestimate the effects of disability.

For data by area, the region used is where respondents currently live, not where they grew up. Because of this, the percentages may reflect graduates moving in from, or out to, other areas.

### Type of data
* Longitudinal Study: Administrative data
* UKHLS: Survey data, weighted using UKHLS population weights
