Data on relative occupational mobility – the strength of the link between people’s occupational class and their parents’ occupational class.

The longitudinal data gives us a stronger picture of the change over time. The other dataset provides rich breakdowns by protected characteristics.

## Summary main findings

### Longitudinal data (1971 to 2021)

The measure of relative mobility compares the chances of people from professional and working-class backgrounds reaching professional outcomes, and avoiding working-class outcomes as adults, using log odds ratios.

According to the ONS Longitudinal Study (ONS-LS), for early-career adults (aged 28 to 37 years), between the 1991 to 2001 censuses:

* relative occupational mobility improved significantly for both young men and young women
* there was a further improvement for young men from 2011 to 2021

Relative mobility among mid-career adults (aged 38 to 47 years) showed little change across the 2001, 2011, and 2021 censuses.

Log odds ratios remained consistently just above 1.4 for men and just below 1.4 for women. This indicates slightly higher relative mobility for women than men in mid-career.

### Other data (2014 to 2022)

According to findings from the Labour Force Survey (LFS), between 2014 and 2022 the link between people’s occupational class and their parents’ occupational class got weaker.

In the 5 years from 2018 to 2022 combined:

* people from the Black, Pakistani, Bangladeshi and Chinese ethnic groups had higher levels of relative occupational mobility than White people
* disabled people had lower levels of relative occupational mobility than non-disabled people
* levels of relative occupational mobility were similar in most parts of the UK

## About the data

### Data source

* ONS Longitudinal Study
* Labour Force Survey

### Time period

* 1971 to 2021 – Longitudinal Study
* 2014 to 2022 – Labour Force Survey

### Geographic area

* Longitudinal Study: England and Wales
* Labour Force Survey: United Kingdom


### What the data measures

#### Longitudinal data and recent data

The data shows changes in relative occupational mobility – the extent to which your occupational class is related to your parents’ occupational class – comparing people growing up in the professional and working classes. 

High levels of relative occupational mobility can be due to high levels of either  downward mobility from the professional class or of upward mobility from the working class (or both).

#### Longitudinal data

Relative-mobility compares the mobility chances of 2 (or more) different groups. Log odds ratios are used to compare how likely alternative outcomes are in one group (in this case people coming from professional-class families) versus another group (in this case people coming from working-class families). We measure the relative odds of people from professional and working-class family origins of achieving professional outcomes themselves and avoiding a working-class outcome.

### Things you need to know

The error bars show 95% confidence intervals. Read more about [confidence intervals](https://social-mobility.data.gov.uk/state-of-the-nation/about-our-analysis#confidence-intervals).

#### Longitudinal study 

The census does not directly measure people’s social backgrounds. It links censuses to identify people’s families in an earlier census when they were growing up and still living at home. This is done for specific birth cohorts. For example, early-career adults were aged 28 to 37 at the time of the destination census and have been linked with data for their parents from the census 20 years earlier. Mid-career adults were aged 38 to 47 and have been linked with data for their parents from the census 30 years earlier. See the technical annex for further details.

While the ONS-LS achieved high levels of linkage across censuses, this method means that recent migrants who were not living in England or Wales in the relevant earlier census cannot be linked. Recent migrants from countries outside England and Wales are therefore excluded.

The class categories are based on the ONS NS-SEC analytical social classes which were developed for the 2001 Census. The questions asked in the census about occupation, and their coding, differ across the relevant censuses.

The ONS states that the categories of NS-SEC are not strictly comparable over time. This is because they come from a detailed list of occupations that is revised every 10 years in order to reflect developments in the labour market, such as new types of occupation. We have grouped the analytical classes into 3 broader classes – professional, intermediate and working – in order to reduce the risk of discontinuities. See section 3.4 of our technical annex.
Future analysis for State of the Nation will use the ONS-LS to analyse results by ethnic group and disability status.

#### Recent data (using the Labour Force Survey)

Survey respondents were 25 to 64 years old. Areas represent where respondents lived when they were aged 14 years.

The data uses the ‘UNIDIFF’ (uniform difference) model, which compares ‘odds ratios’ for all groups with a benchmark group. Values are shown in log form.

Values close to 0 mean relative mobility is constant across all groups. Negative values mean relative mobility is higher than in the benchmark group. The differences were statistically significant from 2019 onwards.

For data by disability status, respondents were asked if they had a health condition or illness lasting 12 months or more, and if that condition reduced their ability to carry out day-to-day activities. This is consistent with the definition of disability in the Equality Act 2010.

### Type of data

* Longitudinal Study: admin data
* Labour Force Survey: survey data
