## Key mobility outcome findings

Mobility outcomes look at people&rsquo;s occupations, educational levels and housing situations, typically in their 40s and 50s.

<hr style="border:none;">

### Education

- Historically, men had higher upward educational mobility than women, meaning they were more likely to have higher qualification levels than their parents. In 1991, 11% of young men from non-graduate families gained degrees, compared with just 7% of young women. 
- By 2011, the proportion of women gaining degrees from non-graduate families (37%) was higher than that of men (32%).
- In 2021, 42% of women from non-graduate families obtained degrees, compared with 33% of men.
- There were similar levels of relative educational mobility between men and women, meaning that the relative odds of achieving a higher degree, compared with peers from different backgrounds, did not differ significantly by sex.


[Read more on educational mobility](/state-of-the-nation/mobility_outcomes/education/absolute_educational_mobility/latest)

<hr style="border:none;">

### Occupation 

- Historically, women had lower chances of long-range upward occupational mobility. In 1991, 25% of early-career men moved from working-class backgrounds to professional jobs, compared with 20% of early-career women.
- By 2011, women overtook men in reaching the professional classes. Upward occupational mobility rose to 36% for early-career women, compared with 34% for men.
- Women maintain a steady lead today. In 2021, 39% of early-career women from working-class backgrounds reached professional occupations, compared with 36% of men.
- Upward mobility increased considerably for both sexes since 1991, driven by the long-term expansion of the professional class.
- The link between parents&rsquo; occupational class and their children's class has weakened more for women than for men, meaning that a daughter's relative chances of moving up the career ladder are now less tied to her family background than a young man's relative chances are to his.

[Read more on occupational mobility](/state-of-the-nation/mobility_outcomes/occupation/relative_occupational_mobility/latest#by-sex-and-ethnicity)

<hr style="border:none;">

### Income

- Among young adults in their early career stage, men and women followed a similar trajectory over time: relative occupational-wage mobility worsened over the first three Census waves before improving in the most recent period.
- Young women&rsquo;s wage mobility remains significantly worse in 2021 than in 1991, meaning parental wages are a much stronger predictor of a daughter's earnings today than 30 years ago.
- Young men&rsquo;s wage mobility has recovered to 1991 levels, with the predictive strength of parental wages rising until around 2001 before falling back to baseline (an intergenerational persistence score of 0.215 today versus 0.220 in 1991).

[Read more on income mobility](/state-of-the-nation/mobility_outcomes/income/relative_income_mobility/latest)

<hr style="border:none;">

### Housing

- Upward housing mobility has dropped for both men and women. In 1991, 67% of early-career men and 65% of women moved from renting to homeownership. By 2021, this upward mobility had decreased to 38% for men and just 33% for women.
- Downward housing mobility has increased for both sexes, and differences between men and women have continued. In 1991, 16% of early-career women moved from home owning parents to renting compared with 14% of men. In 2021, this figure was 31% for women and 27% for men.
- Across both sexes, downward housing mobility is now twice as common as upward mobility, affecting 1.7 million young people in 2021 compared to only 850,000 who moved up the ladder.
- Relative housing mobility worsened after 2001 (and dropped further after 2011) for both sexes, meaning homeownership now depends much more on whether your parents owned a home. This effect is stronger for young women, whose housing status is even more closely tied to their parents&rsquo; than young men&rsquo;s.

[Read more on housing mobility](/state-of-the-nation/mobility_outcomes/housing/absolute_housing_mobility/latest#by-sex)

## Key intermediate outcome findings

Intermediate outcomes focus on the experiences of young people as they move through education and into work.

<hr style="border:none;">

### Compulsory school age (5 to 16 years)

- At age 5, girls have a higher development level than boys. In 2024 to 2025, the gap between girls and boys achieving a good level of development was 15 percentage points for disadvantaged pupils and 13 percentage points for non-disadvantaged pupils.<sup>[Footnote 1](#fn:1)</sup>
- At age 11, girls continue to outperform boys in reading, writing, and maths. For non-disadvantaged pupils, 72% of girls and 66% of boys met the expected standard, compared with 51% of disadvantaged girls and 44% of disadvantaged boys.<sup>[Footnote 2](#fn:2)</sup>
- At age 16, the attainment gap between girls and boys has narrowed significantly in recent years. Among disadvantaged girls, pass rates dropped from 34% in 2020 to 27% in 2025 (and from 61% to 55% for non-disadvantaged girls).<sup>[Footnote 3](#fn:3)</sup>

<hr style="border:none;">

### Routes into work (16 to 29 years)

- Women have overtaken men in post-16 education and training participation. Between 2020 and 2025, women's participation surpassed men's across nearly every socio-economic background, while the proportion of young men who are NEET increased more than it did for women.<sup>[Footnote 4](#fn:4)</sup>
- Young women are more highly qualified. By 2020 to 2025, 74% of young women from higher professional backgrounds held a degree compared to 65% of men in the same group. 
- Conversely, young men from lower working-class backgrounds in 2020 to 2025 combined remain the least qualified group, with 27% holding a degree or above, compared with 33% of women from the same background.<sup>[Footnote 5](#fn:5)</sup>

<hr style="border:none;">

### Work in early adulthood (25 to 29 years)

- Comparing the 2014 to 2019 and 2020 to 2025 time periods, the gap in economic activity between young men and women has narrowed. Young men’s economic activity rates generally went down in all socio-economic groups.<sup>[Footnote 6](#fn:6)</sup>
- Men from lower working-class backgrounds are now more likely to be unemployed than women from the same background (8% for men compared with 6% for women).<sup>[Footnote 7](#fn:7)</sup>
- Despite higher qualifications, women are less likely to secure top jobs. Only 25% of women from higher professional backgrounds secure higher professional roles, compared with 34% of men. Women are instead clustered in lower professional occupations.<sup>[Footnote 8](#fn:8)</sup>
- Young men consistently earn more than young women, and this gap recently widened for people from more professional socioeconomic backgrounds.<sup>[Footnote 9](#fn:9)</sup>
- Young women earn less than young men who hold the same qualifications and socio-economic background.<sup>[Footnote 10](#fn:10)</sup>
- Men overtake women in professional roles by mid-career. While women at age 25 have a slightly higher chance of being in a professional job, by age 44, men from all socio-economic backgrounds have a greater chance than women of holding a higher professional occupation.<sup>[Footnote 11](#fn:11)</sup>
- Women's incomes flatten out earlier. Men's average incomes increase steadily and flatten out later in their careers.<sup>[Footnote 12](#fn:12)</sup>
