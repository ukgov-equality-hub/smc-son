## Key Mobility Outcome Findings

Mobility outcomes look at people's occupations, educational levels and housing situations, typically in their 40s and 50s.

### Educational mobility

- Historically, men had higher upward educational mobility than women. In 1991, 11% of young men from non-graduate families gained degrees, compared to just 7% of young women. 
- By 2011, women overtook men in gaining degrees from non-graduate families. Upward educational mobility reached 37% for women, compared to 32% for men.
- Women maintain a significant lead today. As of 2021, 42% of women from non-graduate families obtained degrees, versus only 33% of men from the same group.
- There was a higher level of relative educational mobility among men than women.

[Read more on educational mobility](https://social-mobility.data.gov.uk/state-of-the-nation/mobility_outcomes/education/absolute_educational_mobility/latest)

### Occupational Mobility 

- Historically, women had lower chances of long-range upward occupational mobility. In 1991, 25% of early-career men moved from working-class backgrounds to professional jobs, compared to only 20% of early-career women.
- By 2011, women overtook men in reaching the professional classes. Upward occupational mobility rose to 36% for early-career women, compared to 34% for men.
- Women maintain a steady lead today. In 2021, 38% of early-career women from working-class backgrounds reached professional occupations, compared to 36% of men.
- Upward mobility increased considerably for both sexes since 1991, driven by the long-term expansion of the professional class.
- The link between parents' occupational class and their children's class has weakened more for women than for men, showing better relative occupational mobility for women in recent decades.

[Read more on occupational mobility](https://social-mobility.data.gov.uk/state-of-the-nation/mobility_outcomes/occupation/relative_occupational_mobility/latest#by-sex-and-ethnicity)

### Income Mobility

- Relative wage mobility has worsened significantly for women in their early career stage. The link between parents’ and women's wages got stronger between 1991 and 2021
- Young men's wage mobility has returned to 1991 levels. For young men, the predictive strength of parental wages increased until 2001 but has recently fallen back to early levels (with an intergenerational persistence score of 0.215 today versus 0.220 in 1991).

[Read more on income mobility](https://social-mobility.data.gov.uk/state-of-the-nation/mobility_outcomes/income/relative_income_mobility/latest)

### Housing Mobility

- Upward housing mobility has dropped for both sexes. In 1991, 67% of early-career men and 65% of women moved from renting to homeownership. By 2021, this upward mobility had fallen drastically to 37% for men and just 33% for women.
- Downward housing mobility has increased for both sexes, and differences between men and women have continued. In 1991, 16% of early-career women moved from home owning parents to renting compared to 14% of men. In 2021, this figure was 31% for women and 27% for men.
- Across both sexes, downward housing mobility is now twice as common as upward mobility, affecting 1.7 million young people in 2021 compared to only 850,000 who moved up the ladder.
- There was worse relative housing mobility (how strongly parental ownership predicts children’s ownership) after 2001. This worsened further after 2011.
- In the period from 2016 to 2020, relative housing mobility was lower among women than men.

[Read more on housing mobility](https://social-mobility.data.gov.uk/state-of-the-nation/mobility_outcomes/housing/absolute_housing_mobility/latest#by-sex)

## Key Intermediate Outcome Findings

Intermediate outcomes focus on the experiences of young people as they move through education and into work.

### Compulsory school age (5 to 16 years)

- At age 5, girls have a higher development level than boys. In 2024 to 2025, the gap between girls and boys achieving a good level of development was 15 percentage points for disadvantaged pupils and 13 percentage points for non-disadvantaged pupils.<sup>[Footnote 1](#fn:1)</sup>
- At age 11, girls continue to outperform boys in reading, writing, and maths.For non-disadvantaged pupils, 72% of girls and 66% of boys met the expected standard, compared with 51% of disadvantaged girls and 44% of disadvantaged boys.<sup>[Footnote 2](#fn:2)</sup>
- At age 16, the attainment gap between girls and boys has narrowed significantly in recent years.Among disadvantaged girls, pass rates dropped from 34% in 2020 to 27% in 2025 (and from 61% to 55% for non-disadvantaged girls).<sup>[Footnote 3](#fn:3)</sup>

### Routes into work (16 to 29 years)

- Women have overtaken men in post-16 education and training participation. Between 2020 and 2025, women's participation surpassed men's across nearly every socio-economic background, while the proportion of young men who are NEET increased more than it did for women.
- Young women are more highly qualified. By 2020–2025, 74% of young women from higher professional backgrounds held a degree compared to 65% of men in the same group. 
- Conversely, young men from lower working-class backgrounds remain the least qualified group, with only 23% holding a degree, compared to 27% of women in the same group.

### Work in early adulthood (25 to 29 years)

- The sex gap in economic activity has halved, but unemployment has shifted.
- Women's economic participation has risen over the last decade, but working-class men are now more likely to be unemployed than their female peers (8% for men compared to 6% for women).
- Despite higher qualifications, women are less likely to secure top jobs. Only 23% of women from higher professional backgrounds secure higher professional roles, compared to 30% of men. Women are instead clustered in lower professional occupations.
- Young men consistently earn more than young women, and this gap recently widened for people from more professional socioeconomic backgrounds. 
- Young women earn less than young men who hold the same qualifications and socio-economic background 
- Men overtake women in professional roles by mid-career. While women at age 25 have a slightly higher chance of being in a professional job, by age 44, men from all socio-economic backgrounds have a greater chance than women of holding a higher professional occupation.
- Women's incomes flatten out earlier. Men's average incomes increase steadily and flatten out later in their careers.
