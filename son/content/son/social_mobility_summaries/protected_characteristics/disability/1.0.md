## Key Mobility Outcome Findings

Mobility outcomes look at people&rsquo;s occupations, educational levels and housing situations, typically in their 40s and 50s.

<hr style="border:none;">

### Education

- For absolute educational mobility, disabled people were less likely to have a university degree than non-disabled people from the same educational background. Among those whose parents had university qualifications, 55% of disabled people had a degree compared with 66% of non-disabled people, a gap of 11 percentage points.
- The strength of the link between parents&rsquo; educational background and children&rsquo;s educational outcomes was similar for disabled and non-disabled people.

[Read more on educational mobility](/state-of-the-nation/mobility_outcomes/education/absolute_educational_mobility/latest#by-disability-status)

<hr style="border:none;">

### Occupation

- Disabled people have a much lower likelihood of long-range upward mobility (3%) and much higher chances of downward mobility (27%) than the population as a whole. Disabled people from all socio-economic backgrounds were also much less likely to enter professional classes.
- In the five years from 2018 to 2022 combined, disabled people had lower levels of relative occupational mobility than non-disabled people. This means disabled people were less likely to move into a higher occupational class than non-disabled people, even when coming from the same socio-economic background.

[Read more on occupational mobility](/state-of-the-nation/mobility_outcomes/occupation/absolute_occupational_mobility/latest#by-disability-status)

<hr style="border:none;">

### Housing

- Among those with homeowning parents, 61% of disabled adults own a home compared with 71% of non-disabled peers, a 10-percentage-point gap.
- Among those who grew up in rented homes, 34% of disabled adults own a home compared with 46% of non-disabled peers, a 12-percentage-point gap.

[Read more on housing mobility](/state-of-the-nation/mobility_outcomes/housing/absolute_housing_mobility/latest#by-disability-status)

## Key Intermediate Outcome Findings

Intermediate outcomes focus on the experiences of young people as they move through education and into work.

<hr style="border:none;">

### Routes into work (16 to 29 years)

- Disabled young people are consistently more likely to be not in employment, education or training (NEET) and less likely to be employed across all socio-economic backgrounds compared with non-disabled young people.<sup>[Footnote 1](#fn:1)</sup>
- Participation in education and training rose from 28% to 36% for lower working-class disabled young people between 2014 and 2019, and 2020 and 2025 (an 8 percentage point increase), compared with a 1 percentage point increase for non-disabled peers.<sup>[Footnote 2](#fn:2)</sup>
- Across every socio-economic background, the percentage of disabled young people aged 25 to 29 with higher education degrees (first or higher) has increased.<sup>[Footnote 3](#fn:3)</sup>
- The proportion of disabled young people from lower working-class backgrounds with a first degree increased from 10% to 15% between the 2014 to 2019 and 2020 to 2025 periods.<sup>[Footnote 4](#fn:4)</sup>
- While disabled people are becoming more educated, non-disabled people in the same groups are advancing even faster, meaning the disability gap is widening in some areas. From 2014 to 2019, the degree attainment gap between non-disabled and disabled people from higher professional backgrounds was 11 percentage points (68% compared with 57%). From 2020 to 2025, that gap increased to 16 percentage points (75% compared with 59%).<sup>[Footnote 5](#fn:5)</sup>

<hr style="border:none;">

### Work in early adulthood (25 to 29 years)

- Disabled young people were less likely to be economically active than non-disabled young people from the same socio-economic background. The disability gap was largest for those from lower working-class backgrounds, at 31 percentage points.<sup>[Footnote 6](#fn:6)</sup>
- Disabled young people from lower professional backgrounds saw the largest increase in economic activity (from 70% to 78%), reducing the disability gap from 23 percentage points to 15 percentage points.<sup>[Footnote 7](#fn:7)</sup>
- In the lower working-class category, the disability gap in economic activity remained at 32 percentage points across both time periods, indicating persistent barriers to employment for disabled young people from lower socio-economic backgrounds.<sup>[Footnote 8](#fn:8)</sup>
- While unemployment for non-disabled people from higher professional backgrounds remained stable at 3%, it rose for disabled peers from 6% to 8%.<sup>[Footnote 9](#fn:9)</sup>
- Non-disabled young people from lower working-class backgrounds were more than twice as likely to reach higher professional roles (10%) compared with disabled peers from the same background (4%). This gap is double the gap from previous periods (6 percentage points in 2020 to 2025 compared with 3 percentage points in 2014 to 2019).<sup>[Footnote 10](#fn:10)</sup>
- Among disabled young people from lower working-class backgrounds, 68% remained in working-class jobs, compared with 45% of non-disabled peers.<sup>[Footnote 11](#fn:11)</sup>
- Even when born into the highest socio-economic group, disabled young people are less likely to maintain that status than non-disabled peers. In the 2020 to 2025 period, 66% of non-disabled young people from higher professional backgrounds stayed in professional roles, compared with 47% of disabled young people.
- With the same undergraduate qualifications, disabled young people earned 8% less than non-disabled peers with identical qualifications.<sup>[Footnote 12](#fn:12)</sup>
