## Key Mobility Outcome Findings

Mobility outcomes look at people's occupations, educational levels and housing situations, typically in their 40s and 50s.

<hr style="border:none;">

### Educational Mobility 

- For absolute education mobility, people with a long-term illness or disability were less likely to have a university degree than people without a disability from the same education background.Among those whose parents had university qualifications 55% of disabled people had a university degree compared with 66% of non disabled peers, an 11 percentage point gap.
- The strength of the link between parent education background and children’s educational outcomes was similar for people with or without a long-term illness or disability

[Read more on educational mobility](https://dev.social-mobility.data.gov.uk/state-of-the-nation/mobility_outcomes/education/absolute_educational_mobility/latest#by-disability-status)

<hr style="border:none;">

### Occupational Mobility

- People with a disability have a much lower likelihood of long-range upward mobility (3%) and much higher chances of downward mobility (27%) than the population as a whole. Disabled people from all socio-economic backgrounds were also much less likely to enter the professional classes. 
- In the 5 years from 2018 to 2022 combined, disabled people had lower levels of relative occupational mobility than non-disabled people. This means disabled people were less likely to move into a higher occupational class than non disabled people even when they were from the same socio economic background.

[Read more on occupational mobility](https://dev.social-mobility.data.gov.uk/state-of-the-nation/mobility_outcomes/occupation/absolute_occupational_mobility/latest#by-disability-status)

<hr style="border:none;">

### Housing Mobility 

- Among those with home owning parents, only 61% of disabled adults own a home compared to 71% of non disabled peers, a 10-percentage-point gap.
- Among those who grow up in renting homes, only 34% of disabled adults own a home compared to 46% of the general population, a 12-percentage-point gap.

[Read more on housing mobility](https://dev.social-mobility.data.gov.uk/state-of-the-nation/mobility_outcomes/housing/absolute_housing_mobility/latest#by-disability-status)

## Key Intermediate Outcome Findings

Intermediate outcomes focus on the experiences of young people as they move through education and into work.

<hr style="border:none;">

### Routes into work (16 to 29 years)

- Disabled young people are consistently more likely to be NEET and less likely to be in employment across all socio-economic backgrounds compared to those who are not disabled.<sup>[Footnote 1](#fn:1)</sup>
- Education and training participation rose from 28% to 36% for lower working-class disabled youth between 2014 to 2019 and 2020 to 2025 (an 8-percentage-point increase), compared to only a 1-percentage-point increase for their non-disabled peers.<sup>[Footnote 2](#fn:2)</sup>
- Across every socio-economic background, the percentage of disabled 25–29 year olds with degrees (First or Higher) has increased.<sup>[Footnote 3](#fn:3)</sup>
- The proportion of disabled young people from lower working backgrounds  with a First Degree increased from 10% to 15% between 2014 to 2019 and 2020 to 25.
- While disabled individuals are becoming more educated, non-disabled individuals in the same groups are often advancing even faster, meaning the disability gap is not always closing. In 2014 to 2019, the gap between non-disabled and disabled individuals from higher professional backgrounds for total degrees was 11 percentage points (68% vs 57%). In 2020-2025, that gap increased to 16 percentage points (75% vs 59%).<sup>[Footnote 4](#fn:4)</sup>

<hr style="border:none;">

### Work in early adulthood (25 to 29 years)

- Young people with a disability were less likely to be economically active than those without a disability from the same socio-economic background; the ‘disability gap’ was largest for those from lower working backgrounds, at 31 percentage points.<sup>[Footnote 5](#fn:5)</sup>
- Young people who have a disability and are from lower professional backgrounds saw the biggest increase, from 70% to 78% economic activity, which reduced the disability gap from 23 percentage points to 15 percentage points.<sup>[Footnote 6](#fn:6)</sup>
- In the lower working class category, the ‘disability gap’ in economic activity stayed at 32% across both time periods, indicating that young people who are disabled and from lower SEB backgrounds face persistent barriers to employment.<sup>[Footnote 7](#fn:7)</sup>
- While unemployment for non-disabled people from higher professional backgrounds remained stable at 3%, it rose for their disabled peers from 6% to 8%.<sup>[Footnote 8](#fn:8)</sup>
- Non-disabled young people from a lower working background were more than twice as likely to reach a higher professional job (10%) compared to disabled people from the same background (4%). This gap is now the double of the gap in previous periods (6 percentage points in 2020-25 compared to 3 percentage points in 2014-20).<sup>[Footnote 9](#fn:9)</sup>
- 68% of disabled people from a lower working background remained in some form of working-class job, compared to 45% of their non-disabled peers.<sup>[Footnote 10](#fn:10)</sup>
- Even when born into the highest socio-economic group, disabled young people are less likely to maintain that status than non-disabled peers. In the 2020–2025 period, 66% of non-disabled young people from a higher professional background stayed in a professional role. Only 47% of disabled people from that same background managed to stay in the professional class. 
- Young people with a disability earned 8 % less than those without a disability but with the same qualifications.<sup>[Footnote 11](#fn:11)</sup>
