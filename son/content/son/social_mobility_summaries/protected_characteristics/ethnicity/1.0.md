## Key Mobility Outcome Findings

Mobility outcomes look at people's occupations, educational levels and housing situations, typically in their 40s and 50s.

<hr style="border:none;">

### Educational Mobility 

- Upward educational mobility increased for most ethnic groups between 1991 and 2011. Among individuals whose parents did not have a university degree, those from Chinese (64%), White Other (44%), Indian (40%), and Pakistani (37%) backgrounds are the most likely to obtain a degree themselves, compared to 27% of White British ethnic group.  
- Opportunities to gain university degrees (relative mobility) have become significantly more equal over time for all ethnic groups.

[Read more on educational mobility](https://dev.social-mobility.data.gov.uk/state-of-the-nation/mobility_outcomes/education/absolute_educational_mobility/latest#by-ethnicity)

<hr style="border:none;">

### Occupational Mobility

- People from Indian and Chinese ethnic groups had a greater likelihood of long-range upward mobility,43% and 45% respectively, than other ethnic groups such as White British (33%).
- People from Bangladeshi and Pakistani ethnic backgrounds had the highest rates of long-range downward mobility, 45% and 40% respectively..
- People from the Black, Pakistani, Bangladeshi and Chinese ethnic groups had higher levels of relative occupational mobility compared to  people from White British ethnic groups. This means that for the former groups parental occupation is a stronger predictor of children’s occupation.

[Read more on occupational mobility](https://dev.social-mobility.data.gov.uk/state-of-the-nation/mobility_outcomes/occupation/absolute_occupational_mobility/latest#by-ethnicity)

## Key Intermediate Outcome Findings

Intermediate outcomes focus on the experiences of young people as they move through education and into work.

<hr style="border:none;">

### Compulsory School Age (5–16 Years)

- By age 11 years, 80% of FSM-eligible children in the Chinese ethnic group are meeting the expected standards for their age. In contrast, only 18% of Gypsy or Roma children reach that same level.<sup>[Footnote 1](#fn:1)</sup>
- As students move through secondary school, this divide often grows even wider. By the time they sit their exams at age 16 years, nearly 73% of FSM-eligible pupils from the Chinese ethnic group are hitting the benchmark level of a grade 5 or above in both GCSE English and maths. Meanwhile, for Gypsy or Roma pupils, that figure drops to just 4%.<sup>[Footnote 2](#fn:2)</sup>
- When looking at attainment of different ethnic groups over time, we observe that while most ethnic groups have seen their results bounce back since the pandemic, some have continued to fall behind. The biggest decline at age 11 years was seen among White Irish children, whose results dropped by 9 percentage points over the last 7 years.<sup>[Footnote 3](#fn:3)</sup>
- At age 16  between  2019 and 2025, the number of Traveller of Irish heritage pupils meeting expected standards nearly halved, falling from 12% to just 7%. White Irish and Gypsy or Roma pupils also saw their results dip during this time.<sup>[Footnote 4](#fn:4)</sup>

<hr style="border:none;">

### Routes into Work (16–29 Years)

- Among 16 to 24 year olds from lower working backgrounds, NEET rates are highest for people from Mixed (26%), Black Caribbean (25%), and White British (23%) ethnic groups.<sup>[Footnote 5](#fn5)</sup>

<hr style="border:none;">

### Work & Earnings in Early Adulthood (25–29 Years)

- Young people from the Black Caribbean ethnic group had the highest rates of economic activity compared with those in other ethnic groups from similar socio-economic backgrounds – 92% of those from lower working backgrounds and 97% from higher professional backgrounds were economically active.<sup>[Footnote 6](#fn:6)</sup>
- Young people from the Chinese ethnic group had the lowest rates – 63% of those from lower working backgrounds and 82% of those from higher professional backgrounds were economically active.<sup>[Footnote 7](#fn:7)</sup>
- Among young White British people, 86% from lower working backgrounds and 94% from higher professional backgrounds were economically active.<sup>[Footnote 8](#fn:8)</sup>
- Young people from most ethnic minority groups earned around the same as White British young people with the same qualifications and socio-economic background.<sup>[Footnote 9](#fn:9)</sup>
- Young people from most ethnic minority groups earned around the same as White British young people from the same socio-economic background, except for those from the Bangladeshi ethnic group who earned significantly less than all other groups.
- In every ethnic group, young people from higher professional backgrounds earned more than those from lower working class backgrounds.
