## Key mobility outcome findings

Mobility outcomes look at people's occupations, educational levels and housing situations, typically in their 40s and 50s.

<hr style="border:none;">

### Education 

- Upward educational mobility increased for most ethnic groups between 1991 and 2011. Among individuals whose parents did not have a university degree, those from the Chinese (64%), White Other (44%), Indian (40%), and Pakistani (37%) ethnic groups were the most likely to obtain a degree themselves, compared with 27% of those from the White British ethnic group.
- Opportunities to gain university degrees have become significantly more equal over time across all ethnic groups.

[Read more on educational mobility](/state-of-the-nation/mobility_outcomes/education/absolute_educational_mobility/latest#by-ethnicity)

<hr style="border:none;">

### Occupation

- People from the Indian (44%) and Chinese (46%) ethnic groups had a higher likelihood of long-range upward mobility than other groups, such as White British (33%).
- People from the Bangladeshi and Pakistani ethnic groups had the highest rates of long-range downward mobility, at 45% and 40% respectively.
- People from Black Caribbean, Black African, Pakistani, Bangladeshi, and Chinese backgrounds had higher levels of relative occupational mobility compared with the White British group. This means that parental occupation was a weaker predictor of a child’s future career for these groups.

[Read more on occupational mobility](/state-of-the-nation/mobility_outcomes/occupation/absolute_occupational_mobility/latest#by-ethnicity)

## Key intermediate outcome findings

Intermediate outcomes focus on the experiences of young people as they move through education and into work.

<hr style="border:none;">

### Compulsory School Age (5–16 Years)

- In the 2024 to 2025 school year, 80% of FSM-eligible children in the Chinese ethnic group meet the expected standard in reading, writing and maths by the end of key stage 2. In contrast, only 18% of Gypsy or Roma children reach that same level.<sup>[Footnote 1](#fn:1)</sup>
- By age 16, 73% of FSM-eligible pupils from the Chinese ethnic group achieve a grade 5 or above in both GCSE English and maths. For Gypsy or Roma pupils, that figure drops to 4%.<sup>[Footnote 2](#fn:2)</sup>
- When looking at attainment over time, most ethnic groups have seen results recover since the pandemic, but some continue to fall behind. The largest decline at age 11 was among White Irish children, whose results dropped by 9 percentage points over the last 7 years.<sup>[Footnote 3](#fn:3)</sup>
- Aged 16, between 2019 and 2025, the proportion of pupils from a Traveller of Irish heritage background meeting expected standards nearly halved, falling from 12% to 7%. White Irish and Gypsy or Roma pupils also experienced declines during this period.<sup>[Footnote 4](#fn:4)</sup>

<hr style="border:none;">

### Routes into Work (16–29 Years)

- Among 16 to 24-year-olds from lower working-class backgrounds, rates of those who are not in employment, education or training (NEET) are highest for people from the Mixed (26%), Black Caribbean (25%), and White British (23%) ethnic groups. <sup>[Footnote 5](#fn5)</sup>

<hr style="border:none;">

### Work & Earnings in Early Adulthood (25–29 Years)

- Young people from the Black Caribbean ethnic group had the highest rates of economic activity compared with those in other ethnic groups from similar socio-economic backgrounds: 90% of those from lower working-class backgrounds and 96% from higher professional backgrounds were economically active.<sup>[Footnote 6](#fn:6)</sup>
- Among young White British people, 86% from lower working backgrounds and 94% from higher professional backgrounds were economically active.<sup>[Footnote 7](#fn:7)</sup>
- Young people from most ethnic minority groups earned around the same as White British young people from the same socio-economic background, except for those from the Bangladeshi ethnic group who earned significantly less than all other groups. In every ethnic group, young people from higher professional backgrounds earned more than those from lower working class backgrounds.<sup>[Footnote 8](#fn:8)</sup>
