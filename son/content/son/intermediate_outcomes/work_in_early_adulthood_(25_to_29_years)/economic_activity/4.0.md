Data on young people aged 25 to 29 years in the UK who are economically active, by socio-economic background.

[TOC]

## Summary main findings

In 2025, 82% of 25 to 29 year olds from lower working class backgrounds were economically active – the lowest percentage out of all socio-economic backgrounds. Percentages in all other socio-economic groups were between 83% and 93%.

In 2025, young people from a lower working class background were 11 percentage points less likely to be economically active than those from higher professional backgrounds.

There is a persistent gap between young people from the lower working group and all other socio-economic backgrounds. While the percentage of 25 to 29 year olds from lower working class backgrounds has gone up from 77% in 2016 to 80% in 2025, it remains lower than the other groups. The gap between the lower working class and the average has not significantly closed, remaining at about 8 percentage points.

### Sex
In the period from 2020 to 2025 (combined), young women were less likely to be economically active than young men from the same socio-economic background. This was most pronounced for those from a lower working class background.

Between 2014 to 2019 (combined) and 2020 to 2025 (combined), the gender gap in economic activity has narrowed. Young men’s economic activity rates generally went down in all socio-economic groups. Young women’s economic activity rates went up in all groups, with the biggest increase in the lower working group (6 percentage points).

### Ethnicity
In the period from 2014 to 2025 (combined), young people from the Black Caribbean ethnic group had the highest rates of economic activity compared with those in other ethnic groups from similar socio-economic backgrounds. 90% of those from lower working backgrounds and 96% from higher professional backgrounds were economically active. 

Young people from the Chinese ethnic group had the lowest economic activity rates – 65% for those from lower working backgrounds and 83% for those from higher professional backgrounds. Among young White British people, 86% from lower working backgrounds and 94% from higher professional backgrounds were economically active.

### Disability
In the period from 2020 to 2025 (combined), young people with a disability were less likely to be economically active than those without a disability from the same socio-economic background. The disability gap was largest for those with lower working class backgrounds, at 31 percentage points.

Between 2014 to 2019 (combined) and 2020 to 2025 (combined), economic activity rates generally increased for both disabled and non-disabled young people from most socio-economic backgrounds. Young disabled people from lower professional backgrounds had the biggest increase, from 70% to 78%. The disability gap narrowed most in the lower professional group, from 23 to 15 percentage points. In the lower working class group, the disability gap stayed at 32 percentage points.

### Area
Economic activity was highest in Outer London, Cheshire and North Eastern Scotland, but lowest in Northern Ireland and Highland and Islands.

## About the data

### Data source
Office for National Statistics, Labour Force Survey (LFS)

### Time period
* 2025 (By socio-economic background)
* 2014 to 2025, 3-year averages (Changes over time)
* 2014 to 2019 combined, and 2020 to 2025 combined (By sex and disability status)
* 2014 to 2025 combined (By ethnicity)
* 2018 to 2025 combined (By area)

### Geographic area
UK

### What the data measures
The data shows the percentage of 25 to 29 year olds in the UK who were ‘economically active’, by socio-economic background.

Someone is economically active if they are either in work, or available for and actively looking for work.

People can be economically inactive if they are:

* in full-time education
* looking after family
* unable to work due to disability or ill health

### Things you need to know
The statistics are from the Labour Force Survey (LFS). They reflect the raw percentages and counts within our data. A percentage point gap or directional change in these charts may not be statistically significant. This is because variations within a sample can often reflect natural sampling changes rather than a trend in the whole population.

People’s socio-economic background (SEB) is measured by the occupation of the main earner in their household when they were 14 years old.

Data is weighted using LFS person weights.

For changes over time, we use a 3-year moving average. The first data point covers 2014 to 2016, and the final data point covers 2023 to 2025. A formal test was conducted for differences in the SEB gap between 2014 and 2025. This was not significant.

Data is combined to get more accurate estimates. Data by sex and disability is combined into two 6-year time periods to monitor change over time:

* 2014 to 2019
* 2020 to 2025

Due to a limited sample size, data by ethnicity is combined from 2014 to 2025.

For data by area, the areas are where respondents lived when they were 14 years old.

For data by ethnicity, the estimated percentages are those for men. Percentages are shown only for people from lower working class and higher professional class backgrounds.

For data by disability status, respondents were asked if they had a health condition or illness lasting 12 months or more, and if that condition reduced their ability to carry out day-to-day activities. This is consistent with the definition of disability in the Equality Act 2010.

The error bars show 95% confidence intervals. Read more about [confidence intervals](/state-of-the-nation/about-our-analysis#confidence-intervals).

### Type of data
Survey data
