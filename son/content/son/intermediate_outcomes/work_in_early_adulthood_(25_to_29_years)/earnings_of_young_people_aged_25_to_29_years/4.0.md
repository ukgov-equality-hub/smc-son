Data on the average earnings of 25 to 29 year olds in the UK, by socio-economic background.

[TOC]

## Summary main findings

In 2025, 25 to 29 year olds from lower working-class backgrounds earned £14.80 an hour on average, compared with £20.80 for those from higher professional backgrounds. The hourly earnings disparity is most significant between the lower professional (£18.30) and higher professional (£20.80) groups.

Over the last 10 years, earnings differences between 25 to 29 year olds from different socio-economic backgrounds have remained roughly the same, with class gaps staying broadly unchanged over time.

### Sex
In the period from 2020 to 2025 combined, young women earned less than young men across all socio-economic backgrounds. Hourly earnings for women ranged from £13.80 in the lower working-class group to £19.50 in the higher professional group, compared to £16.10 and £21.30 for men in those respective groups.

Data for 2014 to 2019 combined shows a clear trend of wage growth across all socio-economic backgrounds, and a widening of the gender pay gap in the higher professional group.

### Ethnicity
Data for 2014 to 2025 combined shows that young people from the Chinese ethnic group had higher average hourly earnings compared with White British young people. Young people from the Bangladeshi, Pakistani, and Other ethnic groups had significantly lower earnings, especially among those from lower working-class backgrounds.

### Disability
Data for 2020 to 2025 combined shows that young people aged 25 to 29 years with a disability earned less than those without a disability across every socio-economic background, with this earnings gap being higher in more professional occupations.

While average pay went up for both disabled and non-disabled workers between 2014 to 2019 and 2020 to 2025, the gap in earnings between the 2 groups remained largely the same, showing that general wage growth has not yet closed the inequality gap.

### Area
People aged 25 to 29 in London regions and those in the south of England had the highest weekly earnings in the UK – people in Eastern Scotland and Tees Valley and Durham had the lowest.

## About the data

### Data source
Office for National Statistics, Labour Force Survey (LFS)

### Time period
* 2025 (By socio-economic background)
* 2014 to 2025, 3-year averages (Changes over time)
* 2014 to 2019 combined, and 2020 to 2025 combined (By sex and disability status)
* 2014 to 2025 combined (By ethnicity)
* 2018 to 2025 combined (By area)

### Geographic area
UK

### What the data measures
The data show the mean (average) earnings of people aged 25 to 29 in the UK, by socio-economic background.

### Things you need to know
The statistics are from the Labour Force Survey (LFS). They reflect the raw percentages and counts within our data. A percentage point gap or directional change in these charts may not be statistically significant. This is because variations within a sample can often reflect natural sampling changes rather than a trend in the whole population.

Data is combined to get more accurate estimates. Data by sex and disability is combined into the following 6-year time periods to monitor change over time:

* 2014 to 2019
* 2020 to 2025

Due to a limited sample size, data by ethnicity is combined from 2014 to 2025. 

Self-employed respondents and those without earnings are excluded. Earnings have been adjusted for inflation with a base year of 2025.

These figures may differ from SON25 findings. This variation is likely due to smaller annual sample sizes, which can lead to higher year-on-year volatility and a larger margin for measurement error compared to multi-year averages. 

Data is weighted using the LFS person weights.

The error bars show 95% confidence intervals. Read more about [confidence intervals](/state-of-the-nation/about-our-analysis#confidence-intervals).

For the over time analysis, we use a 3-year moving average. The first data point covers 2014 to 2016, and the final data point covers 2023 to 2025. A formal test was conducted for differences in the socio-economic background gap between 2014 and 2025. This was not significant.

For data by ethnicity, the percentages shown are those for men. Percentages are shown only for those with lower working-class and higher professional-class backgrounds for comparison, but all socio-economic backgrounds were included in the analysis.

For data by disability status, respondents were asked if they had a health condition or illness lasting 12 months or more, and if that condition reduced their ability to carry out day-to-day activities. This is consistent with the definition of disability in the Equality Act 2010. Among people with a disability, those in work are a more selective group because their inactivity rate is higher.

### Type of data
Survey data
