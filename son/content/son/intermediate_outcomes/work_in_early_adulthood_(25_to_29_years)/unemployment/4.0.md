Data on the percentage of young people (25 to 29 year olds) who were unemployed, by socio-economic background.

[TOC]

## Summary main findings

In 2025, 25 to 29 year olds from lower and higher working class backgrounds had the highest unemployment rate (both 8%) out of all socio-economic backgrounds. Those from higher professional backgrounds had the lowest rate (3%).

In the 3 years to 2025, the average unemployment rate was 5%, up from 4% in the 3 years to 2024 but down from 6% in the 3 years to 2016. Average unemployment rates went up slightly during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Young people from lower working socio-economic backgrounds consistently had the highest unemployment rates. This gap has narrowed in recent years due to rising unemployment rates in other socio-economic groups. 

### Sex
In the period from 2020 to 2025 combined, there were no differences above 1 percentage point in unemployment rates between young men and women from the same socio-economic backgrounds.

When comparing this period to 2014 to 2019 (combined), unemployment has gone down for women from working-class backgrounds:

* from 8% to 6% for young women from lower working backgrounds
* from 5% to 4% for young women from higher working backgrounds

In 2014 to 2019 (combined), young men and women in working class groups had the same unemployment rate (8% each). In 2020 to 2025 (combined), young men were 2 percentage points more likely to be unemployed than young women from the same socio-economic backgrounds.

### Ethnicity
Young people from Bangladeshi, Black, Chinese, Indian, Mixed, Pakistani and Other ethnic groups had significantly higher risks of unemployment than White British young people from the same socio-economic background. For example, among young people from lower working class backgrounds, unemployment was highest among Black African (16%), Black Caribbean (15%), and Pakistani (15%) young people, compared with 7% for White British young people.

### Disability
In 2020 to 2025 combined, across all socio-economic backgrounds, young people with a disability were more likely to be unemployed than young people without a disability. 

Between 2014 to 2019 (combined) and 2020 to 2025 (combined), unemployment rose most significantly among disabled young people from more professional socio-economic backgrounds. While unemployment for non-disabled young people from higher professional backgrounds stayed at 3%, it went up for disabled young people from 6% to 8%, widening the gap. For disabled young people from lower working backgrounds, unemployment went down from 18% to 13%. This helped to narrow the disability gap for this group.

### Area
The likelihood of being unemployed at 25 to 29 years old was highest in the Northumberland and London regions, and lowest in Kent, Highlands and Islands, and Shropshire and Staffordshire.

## About the data

### Data source
Office for National Statistics, Labour Force Survey (LFS)

### Time period
* 2025 (By socio-economic background)
* 2014 to 2025, 3-year averages (Changes over time)
* 2014 to 2019 combined, and 2020 to 2025 combined (By sex and disability status)
* 2014 to 2025 combined (By ethnicity)
* 2018 to 2025 combined (By area)

### Geographic area
UK

### What the data measures
The data shows the percentage of 25 to 29 year olds in the UK who were unemployed, by socio-economic background.

### Things you need to know
The statistics are from the Labour Force Survey (LFS). They reflect the raw percentages and counts within our data. A percentage point gap or directional change in these charts may not be statistically significant. This is because variations within a sample can often reflect natural sampling changes rather than a trend in the whole population.

Unemployed people are defined as not in work but available for and looking for work. Economically inactive people (those not in work or looking for work) are not included in the data.

Data is combined to get more accurate estimates. Data by sex and disability is combined into two 6-year time periods to monitor change over time:

* 2014 to 2019
* 2020 to 2025

Due to a limited sample size, data by ethnicity is combined from 2014 to 2025. 

The data used is weighted using LFS person weights.

For changes over time, we use a 3-year moving average. The first data point covers 2014 to 2016, and the final data point covers 2023 to 2025. A formal test was conducted to test for differences between those from a higher professional socio-economic background and other socio-economic backgrounds. This was significant for all groups other than the lower professional group.

For data by ethnicity, the estimated percentages are those for men. Percentages are shown only for those with lower working-class and higher professional-class backgrounds.

The LFS uses the occupation of the main earner in the household when the respondent was aged 14 years for their socio-economic background.

For data by disability status, respondents were asked if they had a health condition or illness lasting 12 months or more, and if that condition reduced their ability to carry out day-to-day activities. This is consistent with the definition of disability in the Equality Act 2010.

Areas are based on where the respondents were living when they were aged 14.

The error bars show 95% confidence intervals. Read more about [confidence intervals](/state-of-the-nation/about-our-analysis#confidence-intervals).

### Type of data
Survey data
