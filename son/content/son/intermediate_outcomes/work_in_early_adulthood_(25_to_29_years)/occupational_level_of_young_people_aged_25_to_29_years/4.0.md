Data on the occupational class people are in at 25 to 29 years old, by socio-economic background.

[TOC]

## Summary main findings

In 2025, 32% of 25 to 29 year olds from a higher professional background were in a higher professional occupation, compared with 11% of those from a lower working class background. 32% of young people from a lower working class background worked in a lower working class occupation, compared with 11% of those from a higher professional background.

In the 3 years to 2025, 48% of 25 to 29 year olds were in higher and lower professional occupations – up from 36% in the 3 years to 2016. The gap between those from higher professional and lower working class backgrounds in getting into higher professional occupations went up from 15 to 22 percentage points over the same period. 

### Sex
Data for 2020 to 2025 combined shows that young women from all socio-economic backgrounds were less likely than young men to be in higher professional occupations. For example, among young people from higher professional backgrounds, 25% of young women were in higher professional occupations, compared with 34% of young men. 

While young women are less likely to be in the highest occupations, they are more likely than men to be in lower professional occupations in every socio-economic background. Men are often more likely to be in lower working occupations than women. 

Data for 2014 to 2019 combined shows that:

* the percentage of men from higher professional backgrounds in higher professional jobs went up from 26% to 34%
* the percentage of women from higher professional backgrounds in higher professional roles went up from 19% to 25%
* for young people from lower working-class backgrounds, the percentage in higher professional roles went up from 7% to 11% for men and from 4% to 6% for women

### Ethnicity
For 2014 to 2025 combined, young people from Bangladeshi, Pakistani, and Other ethnic groups had poorer chances of entering professional occupations compared with White British from similar socio-economic backgrounds. For example, among those from lower working-class backgrounds, 19% of Bangladeshi and Pakistani young people reached professional roles, compared with 26% of White British young people.

### Disability
Across all socio-economic backgrounds, disabled young people faced persistent barriers to reaching top roles and were more likely to remain in lower working-class roles. Data for 2020 to 2025 combined shows that non-disabled young people from lower working-class backgrounds were more than twice as likely to reach a higher professional job (10%) than their disabled peers (4%).

Data for 2014 to 2019 combined shows that:

* the percentage of non-disabled young people from lower working-class backgrounds entering higher professional roles went up from 4% to 10% – it went up from 3% to 4% for disabled young people
* for young people from higher professional backgrounds, the percentage of disabled people who stayed in professional occupations went up slightly from 45% to 47% – it went up from 59% to 66% for non-disabled people

### Area
The likelihood of people aged 25 to 29 years old of being in a professional occupation was highest in London regions and the South, and lowest in Northern Ireland and Southern Scotland.

## About the data

### Data source
Office for National Statistics, Labour Force Survey (LFS)

### Time period
* 2025 (By socio-economic background)
* 2014 to 2025, 3-year averages (Changes over time)
* 2014 to 2019 combined, and 2020 to 2025 combined (By sex and disability status)
* 2014 to 2025 combined (By ethnicity)
* 2018 to 2025 combined (By area)

### Geographic area
UK

### What the data measures
The data shows the occupation level of 25 to 29 year olds in the UK, by socio-economic background.

We use the following 5 categories of socio-economic background and occupational level:

* higher professional and managerial
* lower professional and managerial
* intermediate
* higher working class
* lower working class

These are based on the National Statistics Socio-economic Classification (‘NS-SEC’), set by the Office for National Statistics (ONS).

### Things you need to know
The statistics are from the Labour Force Survey (LFS). They reflect the raw percentages and counts within our data. A percentage point gap or directional change in these charts may not be statistically significant. This is because variations within a sample can often reflect natural sampling changes rather than a trend in the whole population.

Unemployed people are defined as not in work but available for and looking for work. Economically inactive people (those not in work or looking for work) are not included in the data.

Data is combined to get more accurate estimates. Data by sex and disability is combined into the following 6-year time periods to monitor change over time:

* 2014 to 2019
* 2020 to 2025

Due to a limited sample size, data by ethnicity is combined from 2014 to 2025. 

The data used is weighted using LFS person weights.

For changes over time, we use a 3-year moving average. The first data point covers 2014 to 2016, and the final data point covers 2023 to 2025. A formal test was conducted to test for differences between those from a higher professional socio-economic background and other socio-economic backgrounds. This was significant for all groups other than the lower professional group.

The data by ethnicity shows whether young people are in a professional occupation (either higher or lower professional). Percentages are shown only for people from lower working class and higher professional backgrounds. This is because the number of people surveyed was too small to measure all socio-economic backgrounds and occupation levels reliably. The estimated percentages are those for men.

The LFS uses the occupation of the main earner in the household when the respondent was aged 14 years for their socio-economic background.

For data by disability status, respondents were asked if they had a health condition or illness lasting 12 months or more, and if that condition reduced their ability to carry out day-to-day activities. This is consistent with the definition of disability in the Equality Act 2010.

Areas are based on where the respondents were living when they were aged 14.

The error bars show 95% confidence intervals. Read more about [confidence intervals](/state-of-the-nation/about-our-analysis#confidence-intervals).

### Type of data
Survey data
