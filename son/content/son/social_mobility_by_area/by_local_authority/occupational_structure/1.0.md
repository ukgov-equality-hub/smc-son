Data on the occupational level of 25 to 64 year olds across 215 UK local authorities, measured by the percentage of
people in high professional, lower professional, intermediate, higher working or lower working occupations.

[TOC]

## Summary main findings

### Trends across UK nations
Between the 2001 Census and the 2021 to 2022 Census, England, Scotland, and Wales experienced similar occupational shifts:

* the higher professional class grew substantially, while the higher working class saw an even larger decline 
* the lower professional class shrank slightly, alongside small increases in intermediate and lower working-class occupations

Northern Ireland was an exception to these broader trends:

* it experienced the largest increase in its higher professional class and a unique decline in its lower working class 
* by 2021, Northern Ireland had largely matched the occupational class profile of Wales

### By local area
Analysis of census data over the last 20 years shows that:

* the UK experienced a substantial rise in higher professional jobs and a large drop in higher working-class jobs 
* professional opportunities are unevenly spread across the country, with stark gaps between areas remaining largely unchanged since 2001 
* Leeds has roughly 1 professional job for every working-class resident, whereas Hull has only 0.5 professional jobs for every working-class resident 
* cities such as Leeds and Bristol experienced notable growth in their professional classes, while Birmingham, Middlesbrough, and Kingston upon Hull saw minimal growth 
* growth in the higher professional class in Greater London was matched by a decline of the same rate in its lower professional class 
* Cardiff and Blaenau Gwent are maintaining their respective positions as the areas with the largest professional and working classes in Wales while trends across Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland remain largely stable

## About the data

### Data source
Office for National Statistics (ONS), National Records of Scotland (NRS), and Northern Ireland Statistics and Research
Agency (NISRA), census 2001, 2011, and 2021 (2022 for Scotland only).

### Time period
* 2001, 2011, 2021 (2022 for Scotland only)

### Geographic area
UK

### What the data measures
The data shows the occupation level of employed 25 to 64 year olds in the UK across the 2001, 2011, and 2021
(2022 for Scotland only) censuses by country and local authority.

It tracks how the proportion of people across five occupation classifications, from higher professional to lower working,
has shifted over a 20-year period.

### Things you need to know
Data for 2021 incorporates 2022 census figures for Scotland.

Young people aged 16–24 are excluded to avoid distortions caused by the large increase in higher education
participation over the 20-year period.

Long-term unemployed individuals (unemployed for 12+ months) and those who have never done paid work are excluded due
to changes in how censuses recorded these groups over time.

Data is mapped to where people live rather than where they work. Take care when interpreting areas with high
cross-boundary commuting (such as Greater London).

The ONS revises occupational definitions every 10 years to reflect modern job roles. Small shifts between census years
may reflect these administrative updates rather than actual economic movement; however, comparisons between areas within
any single census year remain fully consistent.

See [section 3.5 of the technical annex]() for more information.

### Type of data
Administrative data
